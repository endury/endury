# Long-Course Triathlon — Half-Ironman and Ironman

Distance-specific training methodology for half-Ironman (70.3) and Ironman
(140.6) preparation. This document builds on:

- [endurance_methodology.md](endurance_methodology.md) — periodization,
  intensity distribution, fitness modelling, taper, strength
- [triathlon.md](triathlon.md) — multi-sport balance, discipline-specific
  principles, brick sessions, fatigue management

Only topics that require distance-specific treatment are covered here.

---

## 1. Race Demands

Understanding what the body must do on race day drives every training decision.

| Parameter | Half-Ironman | Ironman |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Race duration (age-group) | 4.5–7 h | 9–17 h |
| Swim | 1.9 km | 3.8 km |
| Bike | 90 km | 180 km |
| Run | 21.1 km | 42.2 km |
| Bike intensity (% FTP) | 75–82% | 65–76% |
| Run intensity (% LTHR) | 85–92% | 75–85% |
| Primary energy system | Threshold / high aerobic | Sub-threshold aerobic |
| Dominant limiter | Muscular endurance at threshold | Aerobic durability, fat oxidation, fuelling tolerance |

The critical implication: half-Ironman racing sits near the threshold boundary
(LT2), making threshold fitness the primary performance determinant. Full
Ironman racing sits well below threshold, making *sustained sub-threshold
output over many hours* — durability — the central challenge. This difference
drives everything from intensity distribution to session design.

## 2. Durability — The Central Long-Course Quality

### Definition

Maunder et al. (2021) formalised *durability* as the degree to which
physiological thresholds (VT1, VT2, critical power) are maintained during
prolonged exercise. An athlete with high durability experiences minimal
rightward shift of their power-at-threshold over time; a less durable athlete
sees threshold power erode progressively as glycogen depletes, core
temperature rises, and neuromuscular efficiency degrades.

This is distinct from "aerobic endurance" in the general sense. Two athletes
may have identical fresh VO2max and FTP values, yet differ dramatically in
Ironman performance because one sustains 72% of fresh FTP after 4 hours on the
bike while the other can only sustain 62%.

> Maunder, E. et al. (2021). The Importance of "Durability" in the
> Physiological Profiling of Endurance Athletes. *Sports Med*, 51(8),
> 1619–1628.
> DOI: [10.1007/s40279-021-01459-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01459-0)

### Assessment

Durability can be assessed by comparing physiological markers fresh vs.
fatigued:

- **Power at VT1/VT2** after 2–3 hours of steady-state riding vs. rested.
- **Decoupling** (cardiac drift): the ratio of heart rate to power rising over
  time during a long steady session. Aerobic decoupling >5% suggests the
  athlete is approaching a durability limit at that intensity.
- **Off-the-bike run pace**: comparing run pace at a given heart rate in a
  standalone test vs. after a long bike — the "brick decrement" — is a
  practical durability metric specific to triathlon.

### Training Durability

Durability improves through:

1. **Long sub-threshold sessions** — the primary vehicle. Extended rides and
   runs at 65–78% FTP / Zone 1–low Zone 2 develop the metabolic and
   neuromuscular resilience to maintain output under progressive fatigue.
2. **Fuelling practice** — high carbohydrate intake during long sessions
   directly supports durability by preserving glycogen (see
   [triathlon.md](triathlon.md), Section 7).
3. **Heat adaptation** — core temperature rise is a major durability limiter;
   heat acclimation protocols improve thermoregulatory efficiency (see
   Section 9 below).
4. **Progressive long sessions** — finishing the last 30–60 minutes of a long
   ride or run at race pace trains the specific ability to produce quality
   output in a fatigued state.

The concept of durability provides the physiological rationale for why
long-course training emphasises volume at moderate intensity over interval
training: it is not simply about building a bigger aerobic engine, but about
making that engine resistant to degradation over hours.

## 3. Macrocycle Structure

### Preparation Length

| Distance | Minimum | Standard | Extended |
|----------|---------|----------|----------|
| Half-Ironman | 16 weeks | 18–24 weeks | 28+ weeks |
| Ironman | 20 weeks | 24–32 weeks | 36+ weeks |

Shorter preparations demand aggressive phase compression and assume an existing
aerobic base. Extended preparations allow a longer base phase, which is
particularly valuable for athletes raising their volume ceiling for the first
time.

### Phase Ratios

Phase allocation should be *horizon-aware* — the balance shifts with
preparation length:

**Ironman:**

| Preparation | Base | Build | Peak + Taper |
|-------------|------|-------|-------------|
| Short (≤ 20 w) | ~40% | ~40% | ~20% |
| Standard (20–32 w) | ~50% | ~32% | ~18% |
| Long (≥ 32 w) | ~58% | ~27% | ~15% |

**Half-Ironman:**

| Preparation | Base | Build | Peak + Taper |
|-------------|------|-------|-------------|
| Short (≤ 18 w) | ~40% | ~40% | ~20% |
| Standard (18–28 w) | ~48% | ~34% | ~18% |
| Long (≥ 28 w) | ~55% | ~30% | ~15% |

Rationale: longer preparations can invest more time in aerobic base-building,
which raises the volume ceiling and improves the foundation for subsequent
quality work. Shorter preparations must compress the base and enter build
earlier, accepting a less developed aerobic floor.

> Phase-ratio guidance draws on Friel (*Triathlete's Training Bible*, as reported second-hand)
> and Issurin's (2010) horizon-dependent periodization principles.

### Recovery Cadence by Athlete Level

| Level | Pattern | Recovery depth |
|-------|---------|---------------|
| Recreational / high life-stress | 2:1 (2 load, 1 recovery) | 50–60% of load week |
| Competitive age-group | 3:1 (3 load, 1 recovery) | 60–65% of load week |
| Advanced / low life-stress | 3:1, occasional 4:1 blocks | 60–70% of load week |

## 4. Volume Envelopes

### Weekly Hours by Level

| Level | Half-Ironman | Ironman |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| Recreational ("just finish") | 8–12 h/week peak | 10–14 h/week peak |
| Competitive age-group | 10–14 h/week peak | 14–18 h/week peak |
| Serious / sub-elite | 14–18 h/week peak | 18–22 h/week peak |

These ranges reflect the synthesis of Friel (*Triathlete's Training Bible*) and Dixon (*Fast-Track Triathlete*:
time-efficient protocols at 10–14 h/week for Ironman).

### Volume Progression Pattern

Weekly hours follow a sawtooth pattern within each mesocycle: 2–3 weeks of
progressive loading (each week 5–10% above the previous), then a recovery
week at 50–65% of the peak load week. The macro trend across the macrocycle
is a gradual rise through base, a plateau or slight increase through build,
then a sharp drop through peak and taper.

### Long Session Benchmarks

The longest single sessions serve as durability rehearsals and should peak
4–6 weeks before race day:

| Discipline | Half-Ironman | Ironman |
|------------|-------------|---------|
| Swim | 3,000–3,500 m | 3,500–4,500 m |
| Bike | 3–4 h | 4.5–6 h |
| Run | 1.5–2 h | 2.5–3 h |

For full Ironman, the longest bike ride should reach approximately 70–80% of
expected race duration. The longest run need not exceed 3 hours — beyond this
point, injury risk and recovery cost outweigh the marginal training benefit.
Bike volume substitutes effectively for additional run mileage due to shared
cardiovascular and metabolic demands.

## 5. Intensity Profile

### Why Long-Course Skews Pyramidal-Polarised

Pure polarised distribution (~80% Zone 1, ~5% Zone 2, ~15% Zone 3) may
underserve long-course athletes because the race itself sits at or near the
Zone 1/Zone 2 boundary. Athletes need targeted threshold and sweet-spot work
to raise sustainable race-day output — not just a high aerobic ceiling.

The practical distribution for long-course triathlon is a *pyramidal-polarised
hybrid*: a large Zone 1 base (70–82%), a meaningful Zone 2 component
(15–25%), and a controlled Zone 3 dose (3–15%) that varies by phase and
athlete level.

Seiler (2010) noted that Ironman athletes tend toward a pyramidal pattern.
Modern long-course coaching (e.g. Purple Patch, Plews & Laursen) typically
prescribes 15–20% structured threshold work even for full-distance athletes —
higher than the ~10% Seiler originally reported — reflecting a shift toward
deliberate threshold development as a durability lever. The key is that Zone 2
time is *deliberate and structured* (threshold intervals, sweet-spot work), not
accidental drift into "grey zone" moderate intensity.

### The VO2max Window

For long-course athletes, VO2max work is valuable but bounded (see
[endurance_methodology.md](endurance_methodology.md), Section 4 for interval
prescription details):

| Phase | Recreational | Competitive age-group | Serious |
|-------|-------------|----------------------|---------|
| Base | None (strides only) | None (strides only) | Occasional neuromuscular activation |
| Early Build | Optional: 0–1/week | Primary window: 1–2/week for 3–4 weeks | Primary window: 1–2/week for 3–4 weeks |
| Late Build | None | ≤ 1 per 10–14 days | ≤ 1 per 10–14 days |
| Peak / Taper | None | Brief openers only (3–6 × 30 s) | Brief openers only |

Rationale: VO2max development raises the aerobic ceiling that threshold work
builds upon. But the session cost (neuromuscular fatigue, recovery time) is
high relative to the race-specificity payoff for events lasting 5–17 hours.
Residual effects persist 4–6 weeks after reduction (Mujika & Padilla, 2003),
so early BUILD investment carries through to race day.

### Half vs. Full — Intensity Mix

| Aspect | Half-Ironman | Ironman |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| Primary quality stimulus | Threshold / sweet-spot | Sub-threshold aerobic endurance |
| Zone 2 (threshold) share | Higher (~15–25%) | Moderate (~15–20%) |
| Zone 3 (VO2max) share | Moderate (~8–15%) | Lower (~3–10%) |
| VO2max role | More persistent through BUILD | Concentrated in early BUILD only |
| Tempo/race-pace sessions | Central to BUILD and PEAK | Present but shorter; more emphasis on sub-threshold long sessions |

Half-Ironman training resembles a blend of marathon and Ironman approaches:
more threshold work than full Ironman, more volume than marathon.

## 6. Key Session Types by Phase

### Ironman

| Phase | Swim | Bike | Run |
|-------|------|------|-----|
| **Base** | Technique drills, aerobic endurance, pull sets | Long steady rides (Z1–Z2), cadence drills, recovery spins | Easy aerobic runs, strides, cadence drills |
| **Build** | CSS intervals, threshold broken sets, open-water simulation | Sweet-spot / tempo rides, over-under intervals, long progressive rides | Tempo runs, threshold intervals, progression long runs |
| **Peak** | Race-pace sets, open-water dress rehearsal | Race-pace long rides, brick sessions (bike + run at race effort) | Race-pace runs off the bike, short activation runs |
| **Taper** | Short crisp sets, technique focus | Short rides with brief threshold touches | Short runs with strides/openers |

### Half-Ironman

The session palette is similar but shifted toward higher intensity:

- **Build** includes more threshold and sweet-spot volume than Ironman (2×20 min
  threshold on the bike, cruise intervals in the pool, tempo runs at half-marathon
  pace).
- **Peak** brick sessions are at half-Ironman race pace (higher than Ironman
  race pace), testing the athlete's ability to sustain near-threshold effort
  across both disciplines.
- **Long sessions** are shorter in absolute duration but at higher relative
  intensity — e.g. a 3-hour bike at 78–82% FTP rather than a 5-hour ride at
  68–72%.

### Recovery Week Sessions

Recovery weeks maintain frequency but reduce volume by 40–50%. Key principles:

- Retain one moderate-quality session per sport (e.g. a short threshold set in
  the pool, a sweet-spot interval on the bike) to prevent rapid detraining of
  anaerobic qualities.
- Include a fitness test per sport if useful — this doubles as a quality opener
  and provides progress data.
- All other sessions are easy/recovery intensity.

## 7. Taper Protocol

General taper principles are covered in
[endurance_methodology.md](endurance_methodology.md), Section 6. Distance-specific
application:

| Parameter | Half-Ironman | Ironman |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Duration | 10–14 days | 14–21 days |
| Volume reduction | 40–50% | 50–60% |
| Shape | Two-step exponential | Two-step exponential (slower initial drop) |
| Intensity | Maintained in short touches | Maintained in short touches |
| Frequency | Maintained | Maintained |
| Last long bike | 10–14 days pre-race | 14–21 days pre-race |
| Last long run | 10–14 days pre-race | 14–21 days pre-race |
| Race-week sessions | Short swim (technique), short bike (openers), short run (strides) | Same; may add an extra rest day |
| Target TSB | +10 to +20 | +15 to +25 |

Endury holds taper weeks at **50–75% of peak** and race week at **19–40%**, with session
count held near peak (89–110%) and 53–100% of the peak week's quality minutes kept.
Volume falls by shortening every session, not by deleting sessions. That is Bosquet
et al. (2007): maintaining frequency carried its own significant effect (0.35 ± 0.17,
p<0.001) independent of the 41–60% volume cut, and maintained intensity carried an
effect of 0.33 ± 0.14 (p<0.001) on its own. The planner scores it as
`taper_quality_share` against a 0.50–1.00 band.

A two-step taper typically looks like:

- **Step 1** (first half of taper): reduce volume by ~20–30% from peak load;
  retain 1–2 short quality sessions across all three sports.
- **Step 2** (second half): reduce a further 20–30%; sessions become short
  activation work only. Focus shifts to logistics, nutrition prep, and mental
  rehearsal.

## 8. Half-Distance vs. Full-Distance — Summary Comparison

| Dimension | Half-Ironman (70.3) | Ironman (140.6) |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Macrocycle length | 16–28 weeks | 20–36 weeks |
| Phase emphasis | More Build relative to Base | More Base relative to Build |
| Peak weekly hours | 10–18 h | 14–22 h |
| Longest bike | 3–4 h | 4.5–6 h |
| Longest run | 1.5–2 h | 2.5–3 h |
| Race intensity | Near threshold (LT2) | Well below threshold |
| Primary training stimulus | Threshold / muscular endurance | Aerobic durability / sub-threshold endurance |
| VO2max role | Persistent through Build | Concentrated in early Build |
| Taper length | 10–14 days | 14–21 days |
| Target race-day TSB | +10 to +20 | +15 to +25 |

The fundamental difference is one of *intensity vs. duration*. Half-Ironman
rewards an athlete who can sustain a higher percentage of maximum output for
4–6 hours. Full Ironman rewards an athlete who can maintain a moderate output
for 10–17 hours without systemic breakdown. Both require aerobic fitness, but
they emphasise different positions on the intensity-duration spectrum, and
training should reflect that.

## 9. Heat Acclimation

### Why It Matters for Long-Course

Core temperature rise is one of the primary durability limiters in long-course
racing. As core temperature increases, cardiac output is redirected to
cutaneous circulation for cooling, reducing muscle blood flow and accelerating
glycogen depletion. Most Ironman and 70.3 events take place in warm-to-hot
conditions where thermoregulation becomes a significant performance constraint.

### Evidence

Périard et al. (2015) reviewed the evidence for heat acclimation in endurance
sport and found consistent improvements: reduced core temperature and heart
rate at a given intensity, increased plasma volume, earlier and more profuse
sweating, and improved exercise capacity in the heat — translating to ~1–3%
performance gains in hot conditions. Importantly, heat acclimation also
provides modest benefits in temperate conditions through the plasma volume
expansion mechanism.

> Périard, J.D. et al. (2015). Adaptations and Mechanisms of Human Heat
> Acclimation: Applications for Competitive Athletes and Sports. *Scand J Med
> Sci Sports*, 25(S1), 20–38.
> DOI: [10.1111/sms.12408](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12408)

### Protocol

A practical heat acclimation protocol for the peak/pre-taper phase:

- **Duration**: 10–14 consecutive days of heat exposure.
- **Method**: 60–90 min/day of moderate exercise (Zone 1–low Zone 2) in hot
  conditions (>30°C / >86°F). Indoor trainer or treadmill with added heat
  (room heater, extra clothing) when outdoor heat is unavailable.
- **Timing**: complete the protocol 7–21 days before race day. Acclimation
  benefits persist for ~2 weeks after the last exposure.
- **Hydration**: liberal fluid intake during and after sessions; monitor body
  weight to ensure adequate rehydration between sessions.
- **Caution**: heat sessions should replace easy sessions, not add to total
  load. The goal is thermal stress, not training stress.

For athletes racing in temperate conditions, a shortened protocol (5–7 days)
still provides partial benefit through plasma volume expansion.

## 10. Run-Walk Strategies for Ironman

For full Ironman, strategically planned run-walk intervals are a legitimate
pacing tool used by age-groupers and some professionals. The approach involves
alternating running segments (typically 8–15 minutes) with brief walk breaks
(30–90 seconds) from the start of the marathon, rather than waiting until
fatigue forces walking.

### Rationale

- **Muscle damage mitigation**: brief walk breaks reduce cumulative eccentric
  loading, delaying the neuromuscular fatigue that typically causes pace
  collapse in the second half of an Ironman marathon.
- **Thermoregulation**: walking segments allow more effective cooling at aid
  stations and reduce metabolic heat production during the critical period when
  core temperature is highest.
- **Pacing consistency**: athletes who adopt planned walk breaks from the start
  often achieve more even overall splits than those who run continuously until
  forced to walk.

### Training Implications

If run-walk is the race-day strategy, it should be rehearsed in training:

- **Peak-phase brick sessions** should use the planned run-walk pattern at
  race pace, so the athlete practices the transition rhythm.
- **Long runs** (≥2 hours) in build phase can incorporate the same protocol to
  assess the pacing and fuelling cadence.
- The walk segments are not rest — they are deliberate, structured pace
  management built into the race plan.

This approach is most relevant for athletes targeting 10+ hour Ironman finish
times, where the cumulative fatigue of a continuous marathon after 5–7 hours
of swimming and cycling is the primary performance limiter.
