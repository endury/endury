# Triathlon Training Methodology

Multi-sport training principles that apply across all triathlon distances.
This document builds on the universal endurance foundation in
[endurance_methodology.md](endurance_methodology.md) and covers what is
unique to the swim-bike-run combination.

---

## 1. The Multi-Sport Challenge

Triathlon training differs from single-sport endurance in three fundamental
ways:

1. **Divided adaptation budget.** Weekly training hours must be split across
   three disciplines, each with distinct neuromuscular and metabolic demands.
   An improvement in one sport costs time that could develop another.
2. **Transition physiology.** Performance depends not just on isolated
   swim/bike/run fitness but on the ability to execute each discipline after
   the preceding one — particularly the bike-to-run transition.
3. **Injury asymmetry.** Swimming and cycling are low-impact; running is
   high-impact. Volume distribution must respect this: a cyclist can absorb
   15 h/week on the bike with low injury risk, but a runner rarely tolerates
   15 h/week of running.

These constraints make *prioritisation* — deciding what not to do — as
important as programming what to do.

---

## 2. Sport Balance and Volume Allocation

### General Principles

No single peer-reviewed study establishes an "optimal" volume split. The
coaching consensus, reflected in Friel (*Triathlete's Training Bible*, 5th ed.,
2018) and Dixon (*Fast-Track Triathlete*, 2017), converges on a distribution roughly proportional to
race duration with adjustments for trainability and injury risk:

| Discipline | Race-time share (Ironman) | Typical training share | Rationale |
|------------|--------------------------|----------------------|-----------|
| **Swim** | 7–10% | 15–20% | Over-represented vs. race time because technique demands disproportionate practice; low injury cost |
| **Bike** | 50–55% | 45–55% | Largest race segment; highest volume tolerance; biggest ROI for time invested |
| **Run** | 35–45% | 25–35% | Under-represented vs. race time due to injury risk; quality over quantity |

For shorter distances (sprint, Olympic), the swim share tends to increase
slightly (technique matters more at sprint pace) while the run share may rise
(run contributes more to race outcome, and total mileage stays manageable).

### Friel's "Limiters" Approach

Rather than fixed percentages, Friel advocates identifying each athlete's
*limiters* — the weakest ability relative to race demands — and shifting
training emphasis accordingly. An age-grouper with a strong cycling background
but weak swim technique would allocate more time to swim without matching a
fixed ratio. The volume split is a starting point, not a constraint.

### Practical Swim Allocation

Swimming is the most technique-dependent discipline. Drag increases with the
cube of velocity, so reducing drag through better body position yields more
than adding aerobic fitness. Toussaint & Beek (1992) established the importance
of propelling efficiency; in practice, coaches like Swim Smooth (Newsome &
Young) and Total Immersion (Laughlin) advocate technique-first programming for
non-elite triathletes.

This means swim hours are spent differently from bike or run hours: a larger
proportion goes to drill work, video analysis, and low-intensity technique
practice, particularly in base phases.

## 3. Discipline-Specific Training Principles

### Swimming

**Intensity marker — Critical Swim Speed (CSS).** CSS is the swimming analogue
of critical power/velocity, popularised in triathlon coaching by Swim Smooth.
The concept originates from Wakayoshi et al. (1992), who validated critical
velocity as an index of swimming performance. CSS is typically estimated from
the pace difference between a 400 m and 200 m time trial.

> Wakayoshi, K. et al. (1992). Determination and Validity of Critical Velocity
> as an Index of Swimming Performance in the Competitive Swimmer. *Eur J Appl
> Physiol*, 64, 153–157.

**Phase progression:**

| Phase | Emphasis | Key sessions |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| Base | Technique, aerobic endurance | Drill sets, long steady swims, pull variants |
| Build | Threshold development, race-pace work | CSS intervals, threshold broken sets, open-water simulation |
| Peak | Race sharpening | Race-pace sets, open-water dress rehearsal |
| Taper | Maintenance + activation | Short, crisp sets; technique reminders |

**Key principle:** maintain drill and technique work in every phase. Unlike
cycling and running, where technique is relatively stable, swim technique
degrades under fatigue and requires constant reinforcement.

**Open-water specificity.** Pool fitness alone does not translate directly to
race-day swim performance. For age-group triathletes, race outcomes are often
limited more by open-water anxiety, poor sighting, and inability to draft than
by aerobic capacity. Open-water skills — sighting every 6–8 strokes without
breaking rhythm, drafting on the hip or feet of another swimmer, navigating
mass starts, and adapting to variable conditions (chop, current, wetsuit
buoyancy) — are distinct trainable abilities that should be introduced
progressively from mid-base phase onward whenever water access permits. Where
open water is unavailable, pool simulation (sighting drills with head-up
freestyle, pack swimming, removal of wall push-offs) partially bridges the gap.

### Cycling

**Primary quality stimulus — threshold and sweet-spot.** For triathlon,
sustained power below and around LT2 is the key performance determinant. The
bike leg is raced at 65–82% FTP depending on distance, making muscular
endurance at sub-threshold intensity the central training target.

**Beyond FTP — the critical power model.** While FTP remains the most widely
used single threshold, the *critical power* (CP) framework provides a more
physiologically grounded model of the power-duration relationship. CP
represents the boundary between the heavy and severe intensity domains — the
highest power sustainable without progressive accumulation of fatigue
metabolites. The associated W' (W-prime) parameter quantifies the finite work
capacity above CP. Jones et al. (2010) validated CP as a robust predictor of
endurance performance across durations from minutes to hours.

For triathlon pacing, the CP/W' model is particularly useful: it better
captures why a half-Ironman athlete can sustain power slightly above FTP-test
values (drawing on W') while an Ironman athlete must sit well below CP to
avoid premature W' depletion. In practice, FTP-based training zones remain a
workable simplification — but coaches should be aware that FTP is an
approximation of CP, and that the curvilinear power-duration relationship
means zones derived from a single threshold become less accurate at durations
far from the FTP test duration.

> Jones, A.M. et al. (2010). Critical Power: Implications for Determination of
> VO2max and Exercise Tolerance. *Med Sci Sports Exerc*, 42(10), 1876–1890.
> DOI: [10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181d9cf7f](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181d9cf7f)

**Phase progression:**

| Phase | Emphasis | Key sessions |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| Base | Aerobic endurance, cadence work | Long steady rides, recovery spins, neuromuscular drills |
| Build | Threshold and sweet-spot development | 2×20 min threshold, over-unders, long sweet-spot rides |
| Peak | Race simulation | Race-pace long rides, brick sessions |
| Taper | Maintenance | Short rides with brief threshold touches |

**Key principle:** the bike offers the best volume-to-adaptation ratio of the
three sports. It is low-impact, allows precise power-based training, and
produces the largest absolute fitness gains per hour invested (Dixon, 2017).
When time is limited, protecting bike volume typically yields the highest
return.

### Running

**Central challenge — durability under fatigue.** In triathlon, the run always
follows the bike. Fatigue-resistant aerobic running — the ability to maintain
pace as glycogen depletes and neuromuscular efficiency degrades — matters more
than standalone run speed.

**Phase progression:**

| Phase | Emphasis | Key sessions |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| Base | Aerobic running, movement economy | Easy runs, strides, cadence drills |
| Build | Threshold and race-pace development | Tempo runs, threshold intervals, progression long runs |
| Peak | Race simulation under fatigue | Race-pace off the bike, brick runs |
| Taper | Activation | Short runs with openers |

**Key principle:** progression long runs — where the final portion is run at
tempo or race pace — are a signature triathlon session. They develop the
specific fatigue resistance needed to run well after cycling. Seiler et al.
(2013) provide evidence that this approach develops race-specific durability
better than uniform-pace long runs.

**Volume management:** running carries the highest injury risk. Most triathlon
coaches cap running volume well below what a pure runner would tolerate and
compensate with cycling volume, which builds overlapping cardiovascular and
metabolic fitness at lower musculoskeletal cost.

## 4. Brick Sessions

### Physiological Rationale

The bike-to-run transition imposes unique demands. Millet & Vleck (2000)
documented altered running kinematics immediately after cycling: increased
forward lean, higher stride frequency, reduced stride length, and delayed
VO2 kinetics. The "brick effect" diminishes with practice — the body learns to
transition between movement patterns more efficiently.

> Millet, G.P. & Vleck, V.E. (2000). Physiological and Biomechanical
> Adaptations to the Cycle to Run Transition in Olympic Triathlon. *Br J
> Sports Med*, 34(5), 384–390.
> DOI: [10.1136/bjsm.34.5.384](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.34.5.384)

### Periodisation of Bricks

| Phase | Brick type | Purpose |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| Base | Rarely; if included, short transition runs (10–15 min easy) after key bike sessions | Introduce neuromuscular adaptation without adding load |
| Build | Moderate bricks: 60–90 min bike + 20–40 min run at progressive intensity | Develop metabolic and neuromuscular transition capacity |
| Peak | Race-simulation bricks: race-duration bike + race-pace run | Full rehearsal of pacing strategy, nutrition, and transitions |
| Taper | Short activation bricks only | Maintain neuromuscular patterns without fatigue |

**Key principle:** bricks are a *specificity* tool, not a volume tool. The
training stimulus is the transition itself, not the accumulated duration.
A 90-minute bike followed by a 20-minute tempo run is more valuable than a
3-hour bike followed by a 60-minute easy jog.

## 5. Multi-Sport Fatigue Management

### Cross-Discipline Fatigue Interaction

Training stress in one discipline affects recovery for others. A hard bike
interval session compromises the following day's run quality, and vice versa.
This creates scheduling constraints that single-sport athletes don't face.

**Practical guidelines:**

- **Separate key sessions** by at least 24 hours across different sports when
  possible. Two quality sessions on the same day is acceptable if one is
  swim-based (lower systemic fatigue).
- **Hard-easy alternation** applies across the combined load, not within each
  sport independently. A hard bike day followed by a hard run day is a
  two-day hard block, regardless of which muscle groups are involved.
- **Swimming as active recovery.** Low-intensity, technique-focused swim
  sessions can serve as active recovery between hard bike/run days without
  significantly adding to systemic fatigue.

### Per-Sport Load Tracking

Because swim, bike, and run stress different systems, monitoring aggregate
training load alone can mask sport-specific overreaching. A per-sport CTL/ATL
breakdown reveals whether one discipline is accumulating disproportionate
fatigue — e.g. an athlete whose run ATL is spiking while swim and bike remain
stable may be approaching a running injury.

## 6. Strength Training for Triathletes

The general case for endurance strength training is covered in
[endurance_methodology.md](endurance_methodology.md), Section 7. For
triathletes, two additional considerations apply:

1. **Exercise selection should address all three sports.** Compound lower-body
   movements (squats, deadlifts, lunges) benefit both cycling and running.
   Single-leg work (step-ups, Bulgarian split squats) addresses running-specific
   stability. Upper-body pulling (lat pulldowns, rows) supports swim propulsion.
   Core stability work underpins all three.

2. **Scheduling is tighter.** With three sports competing for training time,
   strength work is often the first casualty. Prioritise maintenance (1–2
   sessions/week in Build/Peak) over development, and use sport-specific
   inserts (hill repeats on the bike, strides on the run) as low-cost
   neuromuscular substitutes when gym access is constrained.

## 7. Nutrition and Fuelling Considerations

While detailed nutrition programming is outside the scope of this training
methodology document, fuelling practice is so deeply intertwined with
endurance training structure — and has evolved so substantially — that it
warrants treatment as a training variable.

### The High-Carbohydrate Revolution

Race fuelling targets have increased dramatically. Traditional guidance of
~60 g/h carbohydrate has been superseded by evidence that **90–120 g/h** using
2:1 or 1:0.8 glucose-to-fructose blends is both tolerable and performance-
enhancing for events exceeding 2.5 hours. Jeukendrup (2014) established that
multiple transportable carbohydrates (glucose via SGLT1 + fructose via GLUT5)
bypass the single-transporter saturation limit, and King et al. (2022)
confirmed real-world benefits at the highest intake rates.

> Jeukendrup, A.E. (2014). A Step Towards Personalized Sports Nutrition:
> Carbohydrate Intake During Exercise. *Sports Med*, 44(S1), S25–S33.
> DOI: [10.1007/s40279-014-0148-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-014-0148-z)

> King, A.J. et al. (2022). Carbohydrate Dose Influences Liver and Muscle
> Glycogen Oxidation and Performance During Prolonged Exercise. *Physiol Rep*,
> 10(12), e15342.
> DOI: [10.14814/phy2.15342](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.15342)

### Gut Training

The gut is a trainable organ. Repeated exposure to high carbohydrate intake
during exercise increases gastric emptying rate, intestinal absorption
capacity, and subjective tolerance. Cox et al. (2010) demonstrated that gut
training over as few as 5 days improved carbohydrate oxidation during
exercise. Practical implementation:

- Begin practising race-level carbohydrate intake (90+ g/h) in training from
  mid-base phase onward.
- Progress gradually — start at 60 g/h and increase by ~10 g/h per week.
- Use the same products, concentrations, and timing planned for race day.
- Long rides and brick sessions are the primary gut-training vehicles.

> Cox, G.R. et al. (2010). Daily Training with High Carbohydrate Availability
> Increases Exogenous Carbohydrate Oxidation During Endurance Cycling. *J Appl
> Physiol*, 109(1), 126–134.
> DOI: [10.1152/japplphysiol.00950.2009](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00950.2009)

### Fuel for the Work Required

Impey et al. (2018) formalised the concept of periodising carbohydrate
availability around training sessions rather than adopting a blanket high- or
low-carb diet:

- **High availability** (carb-loaded) for quality sessions, long rides, bricks,
  and any session where high power output is required. This maximises session
  quality and adaptation.
- **Lower availability** (not fasted, but reduced) for selected easy/recovery
  sessions where the training goal is low-intensity aerobic work and fat
  oxidation is a secondary benefit.

This replaces the older binary of "train low" vs. "train high." The key
principle: never compromise a quality session's fuel supply. Carbohydrate
restriction, when used at all, is reserved for sessions where reduced output is
acceptable and the metabolic stimulus (enhanced fat oxidation signalling) is the
goal.

> Impey, S.G. et al. (2018). Fuel for the Work Required: A Theoretical
> Framework for Carbohydrate Periodization and the Glycogen Threshold
> Hypothesis. *Sports Med*, 48(5), 1031–1048.
> DOI: [10.1007/s40279-018-0867-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0867-7)

### Fasted Sessions

Some coaches prescribe occasional fasted low-intensity sessions to enhance
fat oxidation signalling. Evidence for performance benefit in already-trained
athletes is limited, and the pendulum in sport science has shifted toward
ensuring adequate fuelling for the majority of training. If used at all,
fasted sessions should be:

- Reserved for base-phase easy sessions only, never quality work.
- Short (≤ 90 min) to avoid excessive glycogen depletion.
- Followed by prompt recovery nutrition.
- Discontinued if the athlete reports persistent fatigue or declining session
  quality.
