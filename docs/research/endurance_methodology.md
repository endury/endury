# Endurance Training Methodology

A sport-agnostic foundation for planning and coaching endurance athletes,
grounded in peer-reviewed evidence and established coaching practice.

---

## 1. Periodization

### Classical Linear Model

Modern endurance periodization descends from Matveyev's general theory of
training (1960s–70s), itself rooted in Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome.
Bompa & Haff systematised it for Western sport science (*Periodization: Theory
and Methodology of Training*, 5th ed., 2009). Friel adapted it specifically for
endurance in *The Cyclist's Training Bible* (1st ed. 2003) and *The
Triathlete's Training Bible* (1st ed. 1998, now 5th ed. 2024).

The endurance-adapted model divides a macrocycle into four sequential phases:

| Phase | Purpose | Volume | Intensity |
|-------|---------|--------|-----------|
| **Base** (General Preparation) | Build aerobic capacity, movement economy, muscular resilience | Rising | Low–moderate |
| **Build** (Specific Preparation) | Develop race-specific fitness — threshold, muscular endurance, event-pace work | Plateau or slight decline | Rising |
| **Peak** (Competition) | Sharpen with race-simulation work; allow supercompensation | Dropping sharply | High but low total dose |
| **Taper / Transition** | Dissipate fatigue while preserving fitness | Minimal | Maintained in short touches |

The underlying logic is progressive specificity: training starts general and
aerobic, then narrows toward the exact demands of the target event.

### Block Periodization

Issurin (2010) proposed concentrating training loads into focused 2–4 week
"blocks," each targeting a minimal number of abilities (typically 2–3), rather
than developing everything simultaneously. The rationale rests on residual
training effects: aerobic endurance residuals persist ~25–35 days, while
anaerobic and speed qualities decay in ~5–8 days, so abilities trained in an
earlier block remain available while the next block shifts focus.

In endurance practice, block periodization manifests as concentrated loading
phases — e.g. an aerobic volume block followed by an intensity block — rather
than the traditional gradual shift. Many contemporary coaches blend both
approaches: a classical phase skeleton with block-like concentration within each
mesocycle.

> Issurin, V.B. (2010). New Horizons for the Methodology and Physiology of
> Training Periodization. *Sports Medicine*, 40(3), 189–206.
> DOI: [10.2165/11319770-000000000-00000](https://doi.org/10.2165/11319770-000000000-00000)

### Mesocycle Structure and Recovery Cadence

Within each phase, training is organised into mesocycles of 3–4 weeks. The two
dominant patterns are:

- **3:1** — three weeks of progressive loading, one recovery week. Standard for
  most athletes with adequate recovery capacity.
- **2:1** — two weeks loading, one recovery. Recommended for masters athletes
  (40+), those with high life stress, or athletes returning from injury.

These cadences are coaching convention (Friel, *Triathlete's Training Bible*)
rather than products of controlled trials. The physiological rationale is
supercompensation timing: accumulated fatigue needs a deliberate unloading
window to convert into realised fitness gains.

## 2. Volume Progression and Capacity

### Ramp-Rate Constraints

The widely cited "10% rule" — increase weekly volume by no more than 10% — is
coaching lore without strong peer-reviewed support. Buist et al. (2010) found
that a graded running programme did not significantly reduce injury rates
compared to a standard programme. In practice, sustainable ramp rates depend on
training history, sport, and current load.

A more defensible heuristic is to keep the *rate of chronic training load
increase* moderate — roughly 5–10% per week — and to monitor individual
responses rather than applying a blanket ceiling. The key principle is that
volume progression should be gradual and punctuated by recovery, not that any
single percentage is universally safe.

### Recovery Weeks

Recovery weeks serve two purposes: dissipate accumulated fatigue (the "fatigue"
component of the fitness–fatigue model decays faster than "fitness," so a brief
unloading creates a net positive) and allow structural adaptation of tendons,
bones, and connective tissue that lag behind cardiovascular gains.

Coaching consensus on recovery weeks:

- **Volume**: reduce by 40–50% relative to the preceding load week.
- **Frequency**: maintain or reduce only slightly — preserving movement patterns
  and neuromuscular coordination.
- **Intensity**: retain some high-intensity touches at reduced volume. Complete
  elimination risks rapid detraining of anaerobic qualities, whose residual
  effects decay in ~5–8 days (Issurin, 2010).

### Capacity Ceiling

Every athlete has a personal volume ceiling determined by training history,
injury risk, life constraints, and recovery resources. The plan should ramp
from current chronic load toward — but not beyond — this ceiling. For athletes
with limited history, the base phase may need to be extended specifically to
raise the ceiling itself before layering in quality work.

## 3. Intensity Distribution

### The Three-Zone Model

Seiler's landmark review established that elite endurance athletes across
sports converge on a characteristic intensity distribution when training is
classified into three physiological zones:

| Zone | Boundary | Physiological marker | Subjective feel |
|------|----------|---------------------|-----------------|
| **Zone 1** (low) | Below LT1 | < ~2 mmol/L blood lactate, below first ventilatory threshold | Conversational |
| **Zone 2** (threshold) | LT1 to LT2 | ~2–4 mmol/L, between ventilatory thresholds | "Comfortably hard" |
| **Zone 3** (high) | Above LT2 | > ~4 mmol/L, above MLSS / second ventilatory threshold | Unsustainable beyond minutes |

> Seiler, S. (2010). What is Best Practice for Training Intensity and Duration
> Distribution in Endurance Athletes? *Int J Sports Physiol Perform*, 5(3),
> 276–291. DOI: [10.1123/ijspp.5.3.276](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.5.3.276)

### Polarised vs. Pyramidal

**Polarised distribution** (~80% Zone 1, ~5% Zone 2, ~15% Zone 3) describes
what elite athletes actually do across many sports. Seiler's observational work
and Stöggl & Sperlich's intervention study both support it as a high-performing
pattern.

> Stöggl, T. & Sperlich, B. (2014). Polarized training has greater impact on
> key endurance variables than threshold, high-intensity, or high-volume
> training. *Frontiers in Physiology*, 5:33.
> DOI: [10.3389/fphys.2014.00033](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2014.00033)

**Pyramidal distribution** (Zone 1 > Zone 2 > Zone 3) retains a large
low-intensity base but includes more threshold work. It appears naturally in
sports where race intensity sits near or below LT2 — notably long-course
triathlon, marathon, and ultra-distance events.

**Threshold-dominant** approaches — where *unstructured* moderate intensity
displaces easy volume — are the least supported pattern. Esteve-Lanao et al.
(2007) showed that shifting time from Zone 1 toward Zone 2 impaired
performance relative to a more polarised distribution. However, the critical
distinction is between accidental "grey zone" drift and *deliberate, structured*
threshold work. Long threshold intervals (e.g. 2×25–40 min at lactate
threshold) prescribed as distinct quality sessions — with remaining volume kept
firmly in Zone 1 — are well supported and increasingly common in elite
practice. The problem Esteve-Lanao identified was not threshold work per se,
but the erosion of easy-day discipline.

> Esteve-Lanao, J., Foster, C., Seiler, S. & Lucia, A. (2007). Impact of
> Training Intensity Distribution on Performance in Endurance Athletes.
> *J Strength Cond Res*, 21(3), 943–949.
> DOI: [10.1519/R-19725.1](https://doi.org/10.1519/R-19725.1)

In practice, the choice between polarised and pyramidal is not binary. Most
well-structured plans are *pyramidal-polarised hybrids*: a large Zone 1 anchor,
a moderate Zone 2 component scaled to event demands, and a controlled Zone 3
dose for VO2max development. The common thread across successful models is
*intensity discipline*: every session has a clear purpose — easy sessions stay
easy and quality sessions hit their target zone — rather than drifting into a
moderate-intensity no-man's-land.

### Phase-Dependent Shifts

The intensity distribution should evolve across the macrocycle:

| Phase | Zone 1 | Zone 2 | Zone 3 | Rationale |
|-------|--------|--------|--------|-----------|
| Base | 80–85% | 10–15% | ~5% | Aerobic foundation; volume emphasis |
| Build | 75–80% | ~10% | 10–15% | Race-specific intensity enters; quality dose increases |
| Peak | ~80% | ~5% | ~15% | Sharpen top-end; reduce threshold volume |
| Taper | 85%+ | ~5% | ~10% | Volume drops 40–60%; short intensity touches maintained |

These percentages are practitioner consensus synthesised from Seiler (2010),
Mujika & Padilla (2003), and Issurin (2010). No single RCT tests phase-wise
TID progression end-to-end, but the pattern is consistent across successful
coaching systems.

## 4. VO2max Development

### Why It Matters

VO2max sets the aerobic ceiling. For trained athletes, sub-maximal volume alone
is insufficient to drive further VO2max gains — intensities above ~90% VO2max
are required.

> Laursen, P.B. & Jenkins, D.G. (2002). The Scientific Basis for
> High-Intensity Interval Training. *Sports Medicine*, 32(1), 53–73.
> DOI: [10.2165/00007256-200232010-00003](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200232010-00003)

### Interval Prescription

Billat (2001) established that intervals of 3–5 minutes at velocity at VO2max
(vVO2max), with approximately equal recovery duration, maximise time spent
above 90% VO2max. Buchheit & Laursen (2013) expanded this into a comprehensive
programming framework.

Practical guidelines for **long intervals** (the classical approach):

- **Work interval**: 3–6 min at 95–105% vVO2max (or 95–100% VO2max heart rate)
- **Recovery**: 1:1 work-to-rest ratio (active recovery at low intensity)
- **Total work per session**: 15–24 min (typically 4–6 repetitions)
- **Weekly frequency**: 1–2 sessions; never on consecutive days with other
  quality sessions
- **Mesocycle placement**: concentrated in early-to-mid Build phase, then
  reduced or removed as race-specific work takes priority

The **4×4 min protocol** (Helgerud et al., 2007) — four intervals of 4 minutes
at 90–95% HRmax with 3 min active recovery — has become a standard reference
format. Helgerud demonstrated significant VO2max and stroke volume improvements
in moderately trained subjects over 8 weeks with this protocol.

> Helgerud, J. et al. (2007). Aerobic High-Intensity Intervals Improve VO2max
> More Than Moderate Training. *Med Sci Sports Exerc*, 39(4), 665–671.
> DOI: [10.1249/mss.0b013e3180304570](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e3180304570)

**Short-short intervals** offer an alternative with lower perceived effort and
neuromuscular cost. Rønnestad et al. (2015) showed that 30 s work / 15 s
recovery intervals at 100% peak power output accumulate significant time above
90% VO2max while being better tolerated than traditional long intervals. This
format is particularly useful for athletes who struggle with sustained
high-intensity efforts or who need to manage fatigue load during heavy training
blocks. Typical prescription: 3 sets of 13 × (30 s on / 15 s off), with 3 min
between sets.

> Rønnestad, B.R. et al. (2015). Short Intervals Induce Superior Training
> Adaptations Compared with Long Intervals in Cyclists. *Scand J Med Sci
> Sports*, 25(2), 143–151.
> DOI: [10.1111/sms.12165](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12165)

> Billat, L.V. (2001). Interval Training for Performance: A Scientific and
> Empirical Practice. *Sports Medicine*, 31(1), 13–31.
> DOI: [10.2165/00007256-200131010-00002](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200131010-00002)

> Buchheit, M. & Laursen, P.H. (2013). High-Intensity Interval Training,
> Solutions to the Programming Puzzle. Part I. *Sports Medicine*, 43(5),
> 313–338. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-013-0029-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0029-x);
> Part II. *Sports Medicine*, 43(10), 927–954.
> DOI: [10.1007/s40279-013-0066-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0066-5)

### Periodisation of VO2max Work

For events lasting several hours (marathon, long-course triathlon, ultra),
VO2max work is bounded and front-loaded:

1. **Base**: a small dose — strides and neuromuscular activation, plus one
   short interval session (a 30/15 set on the bike) about every other week.
   Elite triathletes spend about 8 % of their preparation-phase training time
   above LT2 and about 16 % in the competition phase (Sperlich et al., 2023), and short intervals give that
   dose at a low fatigue cost (Rønnestad et al., 2020). The dose stays well
   below the build's; structured VO2max blocks still wait for early build.
2. **Early Build**: primary window — 1–2 sessions/week for 3–4 weeks.
3. **Late Build**: taper VO2max to ~1 session per 10–14 days; shift toward
   threshold and race-pace.
4. **Peak/Taper**: no structured VO2max work; only brief openers (e.g.
   3–6 × 30 s).

> Sperlich, B., Matzka, M. & Holmberg, H.-C. (2023). The Proportional
> Distribution of Training by Elite Endurance Athletes at Different
> Intensities during Different Phases of the Season. *Front Sports Act Living*,
> 5, 1258585. DOI: [10.3389/fspor.2023.1258585](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2023.1258585)

> Rønnestad, B.R., Hansen, J., Nygaard, H. & Lundby, C. (2020). Superior
> Performance Improvements in Elite Cyclists Following Short-Interval vs
> Effort-Matched Long-Interval Training. *Scand J Med Sci Sports*, 30(5),
> 849–857. DOI: [10.1111/sms.13627](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.13627)

The rationale: VO2max gains carry residual effects for 4–6 weeks after
reduction (Mujika & Padilla, 2003), so early investment pays dividends
through race day without the neuromuscular fatigue cost of maintaining
high-intensity sessions close to competition.

## 5. Fitness Modelling

### The Banister Impulse-Response Model

Banister et al. (1975) proposed that performance at any point is the difference
between two antagonistic effects of training: *fitness* (slow-building,
slow-decaying) and *fatigue* (fast-building, fast-decaying). This elegantly
explains why an athlete can train hard, feel tired, rest briefly, and then
perform above baseline (supercompensation).

> Banister, E.W., Calvert, T.W., Savage, M.V. & Bach, T. (1975). A Systems
> Model of Training for Athletic Performance. *Australian Journal of Sports
> Medicine*, 7, 57–61.

Busso (2003) refined the model with variable dose-response relationships,
allowing the magnitude and duration of fatigue to change with training state.

> Busso, T. (2003). Variable Dose-Response Relationship between Exercise
> Training and Performance. *Med Sci Sports Exerc*, 35(7), 1188–1195.
> DOI: [10.1249/01.MSS.0000074465.13621.37](https://doi.org/10.1249/01.MSS.0000074465.13621.37)

### Practical Implementation: CTL, ATL, TSB

Andrew Coggan operationalised Banister's model as the Performance Management
Chart (PMC) on TrainingPeaks, using fixed exponential moving averages:

| Metric | Definition | Time constant | Meaning |
|--------|-----------|---------------|---------|
| **CTL** (Chronic Training Load) | EMA of daily training stress | 42 days | "Fitness" — what you've built over weeks |
| **ATL** (Acute Training Load) | EMA of daily training stress | 7 days | "Fatigue" — what you've accumulated recently |
| **TSB** (Training Stress Balance) | CTL − ATL | — | "Form" — net readiness to perform |

The 42-day and 7-day time constants are Coggan's practical simplification.
Banister's original values and Busso's individually-fitted constants differ,
but the fixed-constant version is the standard in coaching tools.

### TSB Zones

Coaches use approximate TSB ranges to guide load management:

| TSB Range | Interpretation |
|-----------|---------------|
| +15 to +25 | Race-ready freshness (target for A-races) |
| +5 to +15 | Fresh; suitable for B-races or key sessions |
| −10 to +5 | Maintenance — neither building nor recovering significantly |
| −10 to −30 | Productive overload; functional overreaching territory |
| Below −30 | Overreaching risk; injury/illness concern |

These are coaching heuristics, not peer-reviewed thresholds. Individual
responses vary considerably, and TSB should be interpreted alongside subjective
markers (perceived exertion, sleep quality, mood).

The zones are absolute, so they do not scale with fitness: TSB cannot exceed
CTL, and a runner at a CTL of 20 a day cannot reach +15 at all. Form over
fitness (TSB / CTL) is scale-free. Endury's planner floors it on race day at 0.27,
scaled to 0.20 for a one-week taper. A 10K tapers for about a week, so it
reads like the one-week tapers.

### HRV-Guided Training

Heart-rate variability (HRV) has moved from research curiosity to mainstream
load-management tool. Plews et al. (2013) demonstrated that daily resting HRV
(specifically the natural logarithm of RMSSD, "lnRMSSD") tracks autonomic
recovery status and can guide day-to-day training decisions: train hard when
HRV is at or above the athlete's rolling baseline, and train easy or rest when
it is suppressed.

Kiviniemi et al. (2007) showed that HRV-guided prescription — where high-
intensity days were assigned based on individual HRV readiness — produced equal
or greater VO2max gains compared to a fixed training plan, with lower total
training load. The practical implication: HRV guidance can improve the
signal-to-noise ratio of a periodised plan by fine-tuning *when* quality
sessions land within a mesocycle.

**Practical application:**

- Use the 7-day rolling coefficient of variation (CV) of lnRMSSD as a
  stability metric. A CV above ~10% suggests accumulated fatigue or poor
  recovery.
- A single low reading is noise; a trend of 3+ days below baseline warrants
  load reduction.
- HRV complements, rather than replaces, TSB monitoring and subjective markers.
  It is most useful for deciding between "hard" and "easy" on days where the
  plan is flexible.

> Plews, D.J. et al. (2013). Training Adaptation and Heart Rate Variability in
> Elite Endurance Athletes: Opening the Door to Effective Monitoring.
> *Sports Med*, 43(9), 773–781.
> DOI: [10.1007/s40279-013-0071-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0071-8)

> Kiviniemi, A.M. et al. (2007). Endurance Training Guided Individually by
> Daily Heart Rate Variability Measurements. *Eur J Appl Physiol*, 101(6),
> 743–751. DOI: [10.1007/s00421-007-0552-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-007-0552-2)

### Limitations

The fitness–fatigue model is a *planning heuristic*, not a physiological law.
It assumes a single training impulse when reality involves multiple stressors,
it uses population-average time constants when individual variation is large,
and it cannot account for qualitative training differences (a tempo run and an
easy run producing equal TSS are not equivalent stimuli). Use it as one input
among many, not as a governing equation.

### Race-Time Prediction (Riegel)

Where the fitness–fatigue model tracks readiness, Riegel's endurance model
(1981) converts a *performance* at one distance into an equivalent time at
another:

```
T2 = T1 × (D2 / D1)^k
```

Riegel fitted the fatigue exponent `k ≈ 1.06` across running records from
world-class to age-group level. The practical spread is 1.05–1.08:

| Athlete profile | Typical k |
|-----------------|-----------|
| High-mileage, deep endurance base | ~1.05 |
| Typical trained runner | 1.06 |
| Speed-biased or low weekly volume | 1.07–1.08 |

Limits worth stating to an athlete before quoting a number:

- **Running only.** The exponent is calibrated on running; cycling (drafting,
  terrain, the power–duration curve) and swimming do not obey it, and a
  triathlon time is not a single power law — three discipline paces plus
  transitions and fuelling.
- **Extrapolation degrades with the distance ratio.** Within roughly 4x of the
  known distance the fit is good; 5 km → marathon (8.4x) is systematically
  optimistic, because the model knows nothing about glycogen depletion or
  musculoskeletal durability past three hours.
- **It assumes equal preparation at both distances.** The prediction describes
  an athlete trained for the target, not a 5 km specialist who has never run
  30 km.
- **It assumes comparable conditions** — course, surface, altitude, weather,
  even pacing — and a genuine race or all-out time trial as the input.

> Riegel, P.S. (1981). Athletic Records and Human Endurance.
> *American Scientist*, 69(3), 285–290.

## 6. Taper and Peaking

### Evidence Base

Two landmark reviews anchor the taper literature:

> Mujika, I. & Padilla, S. (2003). Scientific Bases for Precompetition
> Tapering Strategies. *Med Sci Sports Exerc*, 35(7), 1182–1187.
> DOI: [10.1249/01.MSS.0000074448.73931.11](https://doi.org/10.1249/01.MSS.0000074448.73931.11)

> Bosquet, L., Montpetit, J., Arvisais, D. & Mujika, I. (2007). Effects of
> Tapering on Performance: A Meta-Analysis. *Med Sci Sports Exerc*, 39(8),
> 1358–1365. DOI: [10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0)

### Key Principles

1. **Reduce volume, not intensity.** The meta-analysis found optimal volume
   reduction of 41–60%. Maintaining intensity preserves neuromuscular and
   metabolic adaptations; dropping it accelerates detraining.

2. **Maintain frequency.** Reduce modestly if at all (no more than ~20%).
   Frequency preserves movement patterns and coordination.

3. **Optimal duration**: approximately 2 weeks (8–14 days) for most events.
   Longer events may warrant a slightly longer taper (up to 3 weeks for
   Ironman-distance).

4. **Taper shape matters.** Exponential (progressive) tapers outperform linear
   and step reductions. A fast exponential decay with a time constant of ~5
   days is best supported.

5. **Target freshness.** The goal is a positive TSB on race day — typically
   +15 to +25 for an A-race — achieved through the combination of volume
   reduction and maintained intensity.

### Race Week in Road Running

Race week here is the seven days ending on race day, training only, as a
share of the plan's biggest training week. No study measures it directly;
two lines of evidence bound it.

- **Literature.** A two-week taper that cuts volume 41-60 % on average,
  falling exponentially [6], puts the final week at about 20 % (60 % cut),
  31 % (50 %) or 42 % (41 %) of pre-taper volume. Single-week tapers in
  distance runners sit wider: an 85 % cut (race week ~15 %) improved 5 km
  time [24]; 50 % and 75 % cuts (race week 25-50 %) did not differ [25];
  elite 5-10 km runners keep ~70 % of their regular easy volume over a
  6-day taper and marathoners ~53 % over 14 days [26]. A 158,000-runner
  observational study links stricter three-week marathon tapers with faster
  finishes but gives no race-week ratio [27].

The shorter the race, the larger the race week: short races taper for
about a week, so the race week is the whole taper. Level shows no
consistent effect; beginner 5K/10K plans barely taper because a small
week has little to shed. Endury caps a
race week at each band's top (`RACE_WEEK_SHARE`,
`endury/planner/sessions/session_generator.py`).

## 7. Strength and Neuromuscular Support

### Evidence for Endurance Athletes

A meta-analysis by Berryman et al. (2018) found that adding strength training
to endurance programmes produced moderate improvements in middle- and
long-distance performance (pooled effect size SMD = 0.52). Rønnestad & Mujika
(2014) reviewed the mechanisms and confirmed improvements in both running
economy (~3–8%) and cycling economy (~3–5%).

> Berryman, N., Mujika, I., Arvisais, D., Roubeix, M., Binet, C. & Bosquet,
> L. (2018). Strength Training for Middle- and Long-Distance Performance: A
> Meta-Analysis. *Int J Sports Physiol Perform*, 13(1), 57–63.
> DOI: [10.1123/ijspp.2017-0032](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2017-0032)

> Rønnestad, B.R. & Mujika, I. (2014). Optimizing Strength Training for
> Running and Cycling Endurance Performance: A Review. *Scand J Med Sci
> Sports*, 24(4), 603–612.
> DOI: [10.1111/sms.12104](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12104)

The primary mechanism is improved neuromuscular efficiency and tendon stiffness,
not hypertrophy. This means heavy or explosive protocols outperform muscular
endurance circuits for already-trained athletes.

### The Interference Effect

Hickson (1980) demonstrated that concurrent strength and endurance training can
blunt maximal strength gains. However, for endurance athletes the practical
concern is overstated: when strength volume is kept low (2 sessions/week, few
sets), endurance adaptations are preserved. The interference primarily affects
maximal strength and hypertrophy development, not endurance performance.

### Plyometrics and Explosive Training

Plyometric training — drop jumps, bounding, single-leg hops, box jumps —
deserves specific attention alongside traditional gym-based strength work.
Paavolainen et al. (1999) demonstrated that replacing a portion of endurance
volume with explosive strength and plyometric training improved 5 km running
performance and running economy without changing VO2max. The mechanism is
improved neuromuscular function: faster rate of force development, increased
muscle-tendon stiffness, and more efficient stretch-shortening cycle
utilisation.

For running-heavy athletes, plyometrics may be more time-efficient and
sport-specific than heavy gym lifts. Beattie et al. (2017) confirmed that
reactive strength (plyometric) training improves running economy across
distances.

> Paavolainen, L. et al. (1999). Explosive-Strength Training Improves 5-km
> Running Time by Improving Running Economy and Muscle Power. *J Appl Physiol*,
> 86(5), 1527–1533.
> DOI: [10.1152/jappl.1999.86.5.1527](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1999.86.5.1527)

> Beattie, K. et al. (2017). The Effect of Strength Training on Performance
> Indicators in Distance Runners. *J Strength Cond Res*, 31(1), 9–23.
> DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0000000000001464](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001464)

### Practical Recommendations

- **Off-season / Base**: 2–3 sessions/week. Heavy compound movements (squats,
  deadlifts, step-ups, calf raises) at 3–5 RM or explosive work. Introduce
  plyometrics progressively (2 sessions/week, low volume: 40–60 ground
  contacts per session).
- **Build**: reduce to 1–2 sessions/week. Maintain load but reduce volume.
  Plyometrics can replace one gym session if time is constrained.
- **Peak / Taper**: 1 session/week for maintenance, or cease if logistically
  constrained. Neuromuscular gains persist for several weeks after cessation.
- **Separation**: ideally 6+ hours between strength and key endurance sessions.
- **Sport-specific inserts**: hills, strides, cadence drills, and
  technique-focused work serve as low-cost neuromuscular enrichment embedded in
  regular endurance sessions.

## 8. Overtraining Prevention

### The Overtraining Continuum

The ECSS/ACSM joint consensus statement (Meeusen et al., 2013) defines three
stages of maladaptation:

| Stage | Recovery timeline | Characteristics |
|-------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **FOR** (Functional Overreaching) | Days to ~2 weeks | Planned, productive; short-term decrement followed by supercompensation |
| **NFOR** (Non-Functional Overreaching) | Weeks to months | Unplanned; sustained performance drop, mood disturbance, hormonal disruption |
| **OTS** (Overtraining Syndrome) | Months to years | Systemic maladaptation; requires medical intervention |

> Meeusen, R. et al. (2013). Prevention, Diagnosis, and Treatment of the
> Overtraining Syndrome: Joint Consensus Statement of the ECSS and ACSM.
> *Eur J Sport Sci*, 13(1), 1–24.
> DOI: [10.1080/17461391.2012.730061](https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2012.730061)

### Practical Safeguards

1. **Recovery cadence**: enforce regular recovery weeks (see Section 2).
2. **TSB monitoring**: avoid sustained TSB below −30; limit excursions below
   −30 to a few days, no more than 2–4 times per macrocycle.
3. **Ramp-rate limits**: CTL should not increase faster than ~5–7 TSS/day per
   week.
4. **Subjective markers**: track perceived exertion, sleep quality, mood,
   resting heart rate. Objective metrics (TSS, TSB) should be interpreted
   alongside these signals.
5. **Load distribution**: hard days hard, easy days easy. Avoid the "grey zone"
   of moderate-intensity sessions that accumulate fatigue without providing a
   clear adaptive stimulus.
